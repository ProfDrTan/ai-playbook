1.1

CIA triad

Security has three goals, and a system is only secure while all three hold. Confidentiality means only authorised people can read the information. Integrity means nobody can change it without authorisation. Availability means authorised users can reach it when they need it. Think of your grades: a hacker reading them breaks confidentiality, changing them breaks integrity, and crashing the portal during exam week breaks availability. Exam questions often give a scenario and ask which element failed — identify what actually happened to the data.

Board
Secure  ⟺  C∧I∧A\text{Secure} \iff C \wedge I \wedge ASecure⟺C∧I∧A
Secure if and only if confidentiality AND integrity AND availability all hold.
Ask the room
Q1 Someone changes your grade but you can still log in. Which of C, I, A broke? tap for answer
Integrity. The data was altered without authorisation; you could still reach the system (availability) and nothing was disclosed to you.
Q2 A hacker floods the student portal so nobody can log in during exam week. Which CIA element is hit? tap for answer
Availability. Nothing was read or changed — legitimate users simply cannot get timely access.
Q3 An intruder reads every student’s grades but changes nothing. Which element, and is the system still ‘compromised’? tap for answer
Confidentiality — and yes, it is compromised. Breaking any one of C, I or A is enough; all three must hold for the system to be secure.
1.2

Authenticity

Authenticity answers two questions: is this user really who they claim to be, and did this input really come from a trusted source? A correct password only proves someone knows the password — a stolen one passes too. That is why it differs from integrity (the data is unchanged) and accountability (we can trace an action afterwards). An email that arrives intact from an unknown sender has integrity but not authenticity. The exam definition is word-for-word: verifying users are who they say they are AND each input comes from a trusted source.

Board
Auth=who(u)=claimed ∧ src(x)∈Trusted\text{Auth} = \text{who}(u)=\text{claimed}\ \wedge\ \text{src}(x)\in\text{Trusted}Auth=who(u)=claimed ∧ src(x)∈Trusted
Authentic means the user is who they claim AND the input came from a trusted source.
Ask the room
Q1 Is a correct password enough to prove authenticity? tap for answer
Not fully. Authenticity means the user is who they claim AND every input comes from a trusted source; a stolen password passes the first check falsely.
Q2 A message claims to be from the registrar. What would you check to establish its authenticity? tap for answer
That it truly came from the registrar (a trusted source) — e.g. a verified signature — not merely that it arrived intact. Intact alone is integrity.
Q3 Integrity vs authenticity: a file arrives unchanged from an unknown sender. Which property do you have? tap for answer
Integrity only. Unchanged ≠ trusted origin; authenticity needs proof of who sent it.
Q4 Fill the blank: ‘______ is verifying users are who they say they are and that inputs come from a trusted source.’ Why not accountability? tap for answer
Authenticity. Accountability is tracing actions back to an entity after the fact — it doesn’t verify identity or source up front.
1.3

Access control

Access control is the prevention of unauthorised use of a resource. It always comes after authentication: first the system works out who you are, then access control decides what you may do. Being logged in does not mean you can open the payroll file — the policy decides that. It covers any use of any resource, not just reading data, which is why it is broader than confidentiality. A swipe card that opens the server room for a cleaner is an access control failure, not an authentication failure.

Board
allow(s,o,a)  ⟺  (s,o,a)∈Policy\text{allow}(s,o,a) \iff (s,o,a) \in \text{Policy}allow(s,o,a)⟺(s,o,a)∈Policy
Subject s may do action a on object o only if that triple is in the policy.
Ask the room
Q1 You are logged in. What stops you opening the payroll file? tap for answer
Access control — the policy that decides what an identified user may use. Logging in (authentication) only proved who you are.
Q2 Authentication vs access control: which happens first, and what does each answer? tap for answer
Authentication first (‘who are you?’), then access control (‘what may you do?’). Access control is the prevention of unauthorised use of a resource.
Q3 A cleaner’s swipe card opens the server room. Which security service failed? tap for answer
Access control — the person is identified correctly, but the policy wrongly allows use of a resource they are not authorised for.
Q4 Is ‘preventing unauthorised use of a resource’ the same as confidentiality? tap for answer
No. Confidentiality stops disclosure of information; access control governs any use (read, write, execute, enter) of any resource.
1.4

Passive vs active attacks

Passive attacks only watch: eavesdropping on traffic, reading message contents, or analysing who talks to whom (traffic analysis). Nothing is changed, so they are hard to detect but easy to defeat with encryption. Active attacks change things: modifying messages, pretending to be someone else (masquerade), replaying old messages, or denial of service. Sniffing café Wi-Fi is passive; capturing a login and replaying it is active. The exam phrase to watch for is 'alter system resources or affect their operation' — that is active.

Board
passive: m′=mactive: m′≠m\text{passive: } m'=m \qquad \text{active: } m'\neq mpassive: m′=mactive: m′=m
Passive: the message arrives unchanged. Active: what arrives is not what was sent.
Ask the room
Q1 Is reading someone’s Wi-Fi traffic passive or active? What about replaying it? tap for answer
Reading = passive (nothing changes). Replaying = active (it injects traffic and alters what the system receives).
Q2 Why are passive attacks said to be ‘hard to detect but easy to prevent’? tap for answer
Nothing is altered, so there is no trace; but encryption makes the captured traffic useless.
Q3 ‘______ attacks attempt to alter system resources or affect their operation.’ Give one example. tap for answer
Active — e.g. modification, masquerade, replay or denial of service.
1.5

Server-side web attacks

A server-side web attack targets the web application running on the server. SQL injection feeds database commands through a form; XML injection abuses the server’s XML processing; directory traversal (../../) reaches files outside the web folder; cross-site scripting is taught here as a web application attack too. ARP spoofing is different — it attacks the local network, not the web app. A drive-by download infects the visitor’s own browser, so it is client-side. Web server software is a major part of the attack surface because anyone on the internet can send it input.

Board
▸ Write four attacks on the board, cross one out live, ask the room why.
Ask the room
Q1 Which one is the odd one out: SQL injection, XSS, ARP spoofing, directory traversal? tap for answer
ARP spoofing — it attacks the local network (layer 2), not the web application on the server.
Q2 Why does SQL injection count as server-side? tap for answer
The malicious input is executed by the server’s database through the web app — the server is the target.
Q3 Directory traversal (../../etc/passwd): what is the attacker reaching for? tap for answer
Files on the web server outside the web root — a server-side attack on the application’s file handling.
Q4 Drive-by download vs XML injection: which is NOT server-side? tap for answer
Drive-by download — it infects the visitor’s browser/PC (client side). XML injection targets the server’s XML processing.
Q5 Why is web server software a particular focus of the attack surface? tap for answer
It is exposed to everyone on the internet, so every input it accepts is a door an attacker can try.
1.6

Privilege escalation

Privilege escalation means a user ends up with access they should not have. Horizontal escalation moves sideways: a student opens another student’s account — same level, different user. Vertical escalation moves upwards: a student gains lecturer powers to change marks. The exam definition — 'accesses the restricted functions of a similar user' — is horizontal; 'similar' is the clue. Transitive access is a different idea: gaining access through a chain of trust (A trusts B, B trusts C).

Board
horiz: L(u′)=L(u)vert: L(u′)>L(u)\text{horiz: } L(u')=L(u) \qquad \text{vert: } L(u')>L(u)horiz: L(u′)=L(u)vert: L(u′)>L(u)
Horizontal: same privilege level, different user. Vertical: a higher level.
Ask the room
Q1 A student opens another student’s account. Horizontal or vertical? tap for answer
Horizontal — same privilege level, sideways into a peer’s functions.
Q2 A student account gains lecturer powers to edit marks. Which type? tap for answer
Vertical — moving up to a higher privilege level.
Q3 Define horizontal privilege escalation in one line. tap for answer
A user with restricted privileges accesses the restricted functions of a similar user.
Q4 Transitive access vs privilege escalation — same thing? tap for answer
No. Transitive access is gaining access through a trusted intermediary (A trusts B, B trusts C); escalation is about a user’s own privilege level being exceeded.
1.7

Defence in depth (onion model)

Defence in depth stacks independent layers of protection — perimeter, network, host, application, data — like the layers of an onion. An attacker must break every layer, so the chance of a full breach is the product of each layer failing: three layers that each stop 90% let only 1 in 1,000 attacks through. The layers must be independent; if one stolen password opens all of them, they fail together. Planning the layers means valuing your assets, assessing threats, grouping systems by sensitivity, then separating layers with firewalls, a DMZ and VLANs. Deep layering plus a small attack surface gives the lowest risk.

P(breach)=∏i=1Lpi(0.13=0.001)P(\text{breach}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p_i \quad (0.1^3 = 0.001)P(breach)=∏i=1L​pi​(0.13=0.001)
The chance of a breach is the product of each layer’s failure chance: one in a thousand here.
Ask the room
Q1 Three layers each stop 90% of attacks. What fraction gets all the way in? tap for answer
0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.001 — one in a thousand. That is why layers matter.
Q2 What steps decide which layers you need? tap for answer
Identify assets and their value, assess threats and risks, then place controls per layer (perimeter, network, host, application, data) and decide how layers are separated (firewalls, DMZ, VLANs).
Q3 ‘More layers always means more security.’ Agree? tap for answer
Not automatically — layers must be independent. If one stolen password opens every layer, the layers fail together.
1.8

Malware

Malware is any software written to harm a system. A virus attaches itself to a host file and needs the user to run it; a worm spreads across the network by itself; a Trojan pretends to be useful software; ransomware encrypts your files and demands payment. Each has a matching defence: anti-malware scanning and not opening untrusted files for viruses, prompt patching and network segmentation for worms, user awareness for Trojans, and offline backups for ransomware. The short-answer question asks for two types and how to mitigate each — name the type, say how it spreads, then give its defence.

Board
▸ Virus needs a host and a user click; a worm spreads by itself. Ask for one mitigation each.
Ask the room
Q1 What does a worm do that a virus cannot? tap for answer
Spread by itself across the network. A virus needs a host file and usually a user action to run.
Q2 Give one mitigation for a virus and one for a worm. tap for answer
Virus: anti-malware scanning plus not opening untrusted attachments. Worm: prompt patching and firewalls/segmentation to block its spread.
