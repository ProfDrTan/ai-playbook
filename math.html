2Switch attacks: MAC flooding, MAC impersonation, ARP poisoning, port mirroring, network tap.
Ask: Fill a switch’s address table with fake MACs. What does the switch turn into?
\(|\text{MAC table}| > \text{capacity} \Rightarrow \text{switch} \to \text{hub}\)
Once the address table overflows, the switch behaves like a hub and broadcasts everything.

The gateway’s IP address now maps to the attacker’s MAC address.

3Salt and pepper: stop identical passwords giving identical hashes, and defeat precomputed tables.
Ask: Two users both pick “password123”. Without salt, what does the attacker see?
\(\text{stored} = \big(s,\ H(s \,\|\, pw \,\|\, p)\big),\ s \text{ random per user},\ p \text{ secret}\)

4Correctness: with the same key on both sides, decryption undoes encryption.
Ask: If decryption did not give m back, what would be broken?
\(\forall k,\forall m:\ D_k(E_k(m)) = m\)
For every key k and every message m, decrypting with k what was encrypted with k gives back m.

4Brute force: on average you search half the keyspace. DES has 256 keys.
Ask: Why only half the keyspace on average?
\(\mathbb{E}[\text{tries}] = \tfrac{|\mathcal{K}|}{2} = 2^{n-1}\)
The expected number of tries is the size of the keyspace over two, which is two to the n minus one.
W4-3
4Reusing a stream key cancels the keystream and leaks both messages.
Ask: WEP used a strong cipher. So why did it fall?
\(c \oplus c' = (m \oplus z) \oplus (m' \oplus z) = m \oplus m'\)
c XOR c-prime equals m XOR m-prime: the keystream z cancels out.
—
4CTR mode turns a block cipher into a stream cipher (so does CFB).
Ask: Why can CTR encrypt block 100 before block 1?
\(C_i = P_i \oplus E_k(\text{ctr}_i)\)
Ciphertext block i equals plaintext block i XOR the encryption of counter i.
W4-2
5Successful decryption does not prove the message is unaltered.
Ask: I swapped two encrypted blocks and it still decrypted. Is the message authentic?
\(D_k(c') \text{ decrypts} \;\not\Rightarrow\; m' = m\)
That c-prime decrypts does not imply that m-prime equals m.

5Two tag types: a hash has one input; a MAC has two (key + message).
Ask: If a hash needs no key, how can it prove who sent the message?
\(MD = H(M) \quad\text{vs}\quad T = F(K, M)\)
The digest equals H of M, versus the tag equals F of K and M.

5Any length in, fixed length out: reversible in principle, infeasible in practice.
Ask: If outputs are shorter than inputs, must collisions exist?
\(H: \{0,1\}^{*} \rightarrow \{0,1\}^{n}\)
H maps bit strings of any length to bit strings of length n.

5XOR hash: order doesn’t matter, so swapping blocks gives the same hash — a collision.
Ask: Give me a different message with the same XOR hash as 0100 1011 1101 0011.
\(h = B_1 \oplus B_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus B_m,\quad B_1 \oplus B_2 = B_2 \oplus B_1\)
h equals B-one XOR B-two XOR … B-m; and B-one XOR B-two equals B-two XOR B-one.

5Birthday paradox: finding any collision costs far less than matching a given hash.
Ask: How many people before two probably share a birthday?
\(1 - \prod_{i=0}^{22}\tfrac{365-i}{365} \approx 0.507;\quad 2^{n/2} \ll 2^{n}\)
One minus the product, for i from 0 to 22, of 365 minus i over 365, is about 0.507; and two to the n over two is far smaller than two to the n.
—
5HMAC: hash twice, with the key disguised two different ways.
Ask: Why not just hash the key and the message once?
\(H\big((K^{+}\!\oplus \text{opad}) \,\|\, H((K^{+}\!\oplus \text{ipad}) \,\|\, M)\big)\)
H of: K-plus XOR opad, joined to H of K-plus XOR ipad joined to M.

Decrypting with the secret key what was encrypted with the public key gives back m.

5Diffie-Hellman: both sides reach the same key without sending it — but nothing proves who is on the other end.
Ask: Can an attacker in the middle run two exchanges at once?
\(K = (\alpha^{b})^{a} = (\alpha^{a})^{b} = \alpha^{ab} \bmod q\)
K equals alpha to the b, raised to the a, equals alpha to the a, raised to the b, equals alpha to the a-b, mod q.
W5-3
5RSA: e and d are inverses modulo φ(n). Example: 7 × 23 = 161 ≡ 1 (mod 160).
Ask: Factor 187. Now factor a 600-digit number.
\(ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)},\quad C = M^{e},\ M = C^{d} \bmod n\)
e times d is congruent to 1 modulo phi of n; C equals M to the e, and M equals C to the d, mod n.
—
5Digital signature: sign the hash with the private key; anyone checks with the public key.
Ask: What does a signature give that a MAC cannot?
\(S = H(M)^{d} \bmod n,\quad \text{accept iff } S^{e} \bmod n = H(M)\)
S equals H of M to the d, mod n; accept if and only if S to the e, mod n, equals H of M.

6Symmetric key count explodes: every pair needs its own key.
Ask: 15 people, one key per pair. How many keys?
\(N = \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \qquad n=15 \Rightarrow 105\)
N equals n choose 2, which is n times n minus one over two; fifteen people need 105 keys.
