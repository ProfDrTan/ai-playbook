Math

1.1

CIA triad

Security has three goals, and a system is only secure while all three hold. Confidentiality means only authorised people can read the information. Integrity means nobody can change it without authorisation. Availability means authorised users can reach it when they need it. Think of your grades: a hacker reading them breaks confidentiality, changing them breaks integrity, and crashing the portal during exam week breaks availability. Exam questions often give a scenario and ask which element failed — identify what actually happened to the data.

Board
Secure  ⟺  C∧I∧A\text{Secure} \iff C \wedge I \wedge ASecure⟺C∧I∧A
Secure if and only if confidentiality AND integrity AND availability all hold.
Ask the room
Q1 Someone changes your grade but you can still log in. Which of C, I, A broke?
Integrity. The data was altered without authorisation; you could still reach the system (availability) and nothing was disclosed to you.
Q2 A hacker floods the student portal so nobody can log in during exam week. Which CIA element is hit?
Availability. Nothing was read or changed — legitimate users simply cannot get timely access.
Q3 An intruder reads every student’s grades but changes nothing. Which element, and is the system still ‘compromised’?
Confidentiality — and yes, it is compromised. Breaking any one of C, I or A is enough; all three must hold for the system to be secure.
1.2

Authenticity

Authenticity answers two questions: is this user really who they claim to be, and did this input really come from a trusted source? A correct password only proves someone knows the password — a stolen one passes too. That is why it differs from integrity (the data is unchanged) and accountability (we can trace an action afterwards). An email that arrives intact from an unknown sender has integrity but not authenticity. The exam definition is word-for-word: verifying users are who they say they are AND each input comes from a trusted source.

Board
Auth=who(u)=claimed ∧ src(x)∈Trusted\text{Auth} = \text{who}(u)=\text{claimed}\ \wedge\ \text{src}(x)\in\text{Trusted}Auth=who(u)=claimed ∧ src(x)∈Trusted
Authentic means the user is who they claim AND the input came from a trusted source.
Ask the room
Q1 Is a correct password enough to prove authenticity?
Not fully. Authenticity means the user is who they claim AND every input comes from a trusted source; a stolen password passes the first check falsely.
Q2 A message claims to be from the registrar. What would you check to establish its authenticity?
That it truly came from the registrar (a trusted source) — e.g. a verified signature — not merely that it arrived intact. Intact alone is integrity.
Q3 Integrity vs authenticity: a file arrives unchanged from an unknown sender. Which property do you have?
Integrity only. Unchanged ≠ trusted origin; authenticity needs proof of who sent it.
Q4 Fill the blank: ‘______ is verifying users are who they say they are and that inputs come from a trusted source.’ Why not accountability?
Authenticity. Accountability is tracing actions back to an entity after the fact — it doesn’t verify identity or source up front.
1.3

Access control

Access control is the prevention of unauthorised use of a resource. It always comes after authentication: first the system works out who you are, then access control decides what you may do. Being logged in does not mean you can open the payroll file — the policy decides that. It covers any use of any resource, not just reading data, which is why it is broader than confidentiality. A swipe card that opens the server room for a cleaner is an access control failure, not an authentication failure.

Board
allow(s,o,a)  ⟺  (s,o,a)∈Policy\text{allow}(s,o,a) \iff (s,o,a) \in \text{Policy}allow(s,o,a)⟺(s,o,a)∈Policy
Subject s may do action a on object o only if that triple is in the policy.
Ask the room
Q1 You are logged in. What stops you opening the payroll file?
Access control — the policy that decides what an identified user may use. Logging in (authentication) only proved who you are.
Q2 Authentication vs access control: which happens first, and what does each answer?
Authentication first (‘who are you?’), then access control (‘what may you do?’). Access control is the prevention of unauthorised use of a resource.
Q3 A cleaner’s swipe card opens the server room. Which security service failed?
Access control — the person is identified correctly, but the policy wrongly allows use of a resource they are not authorised for.
Q4 Is ‘preventing unauthorised use of a resource’ the same as confidentiality?
No. Confidentiality stops disclosure of information; access control governs any use (read, write, execute, enter) of any resource.
1.4

Passive vs active attacks

Passive attacks only watch: eavesdropping on traffic, reading message contents, or analysing who talks to whom (traffic analysis). Nothing is changed, so they are hard to detect but easy to defeat with encryption. Active attacks change things: modifying messages, pretending to be someone else (masquerade), replaying old messages, or denial of service. Sniffing café Wi-Fi is passive; capturing a login and replaying it is active. The exam phrase to watch for is 'alter system resources or affect their operation' — that is active.

Board
passive: m′=mactive: m′≠m\text{passive: } m'=m \qquad \text{active: } m'\neq mpassive: m′=mactive: m′=m
Passive: the message arrives unchanged. Active: what arrives is not what was sent.
Ask the room
Q1 Is reading someone’s Wi-Fi traffic passive or active? What about replaying it?
Reading = passive (nothing changes). Replaying = active (it injects traffic and alters what the system receives).
Q2 Why are passive attacks said to be ‘hard to detect but easy to prevent’?
Nothing is altered, so there is no trace; but encryption makes the captured traffic useless.
Q3 ‘______ attacks attempt to alter system resources or affect their operation.’ Give one example.
Active — e.g. modification, masquerade, replay or denial of service.
Q4 ‘Data integrity relates to passive attacks.’ Right or wrong?
Wrong. Integrity protects against active attacks — modification, insertion, replay. Passive attacks only read, which is what confidentiality guards against.
1.5

Server-side web attacks

A server-side web attack targets the web application running on the server. SQL injection feeds database commands through a form; XML injection abuses the server’s XML processing; directory traversal (../../) reaches files outside the web folder; cross-site scripting is taught here as a web application attack too. ARP spoofing is different — it attacks the local network, not the web app. A drive-by download infects the visitor’s own browser, so it is client-side. Web server software is a major part of the attack surface because anyone on the internet can send it input.

Board
▸ Write four attacks on the board, cross one out live, ask the room why.
Ask the room
Q1 Which one is the odd one out: SQL injection, XSS, ARP spoofing, directory traversal?
ARP spoofing — it attacks the local network (layer 2), not the web application on the server.
Q2 Why does SQL injection count as server-side?
The malicious input is executed by the server’s database through the web app — the server is the target.
Q3 Directory traversal (../../etc/passwd): what is the attacker reaching for?
Files on the web server outside the web root — a server-side attack on the application’s file handling.
Q4 Drive-by download vs XML injection: which is NOT server-side?
Drive-by download — it infects the visitor’s browser/PC (client side). XML injection targets the server’s XML processing.
Q5 Why is web server software a particular focus of the attack surface?
It is exposed to everyone on the internet, so every input it accepts is a door an attacker can try.
1.6

Privilege escalation

Privilege escalation means a user ends up with access they should not have. Horizontal escalation moves sideways: a student opens another student’s account — same level, different user. Vertical escalation moves upwards: a student gains lecturer powers to change marks. The exam definition — 'accesses the restricted functions of a similar user' — is horizontal; 'similar' is the clue. Transitive access is a different idea: gaining access through a chain of trust (A trusts B, B trusts C).

Board
horiz: L(u′)=L(u)vert: L(u′)>L(u)\text{horiz: } L(u')=L(u) \qquad \text{vert: } L(u')>L(u)horiz: L(u′)=L(u)vert: L(u′)>L(u)
Horizontal: same privilege level, different user. Vertical: a higher level.
Ask the room
Q1 A student opens another student’s account. Horizontal or vertical?
Horizontal — same privilege level, sideways into a peer’s functions.
Q2 A student account gains lecturer powers to edit marks. Which type?
Vertical — moving up to a higher privilege level.
Q3 Define horizontal privilege escalation in one line.
A user with restricted privileges accesses the restricted functions of a similar user.
Q4 Transitive access vs privilege escalation — same thing?
No. Transitive access is gaining access through a trusted intermediary (A trusts B, B trusts C); escalation is about a user’s own privilege level being exceeded.
1.7

Defence in depth (onion model)

Defence in depth stacks independent layers of protection — perimeter, network, host, application, data — like the layers of an onion. An attacker must break every layer, so the chance of a full breach is the product of each layer failing: three layers that each stop 90% let only 1 in 1,000 attacks through. The layers must be independent; if one stolen password opens all of them, they fail together. Planning the layers means valuing your assets, assessing threats, grouping systems by sensitivity, then separating layers with firewalls, a DMZ and VLANs. Deep layering plus a small attack surface gives the lowest risk.

Board
P(breach)=∏i=1Lpi(0.13=0.001)P(\text{breach}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p_i \quad (0.1^3 = 0.001)P(breach)=∏i=1L​pi​(0.13=0.001)
The chance of a breach is the product of each layer’s failure chance: one in a thousand here.
Ask the room
Q1 Three layers each stop 90% of attacks. What fraction gets all the way in?
0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.001 — one in a thousand. That is why layers matter.
Q2 What steps decide which layers you need?
Identify assets and their value, assess threats and risks, then place controls per layer (perimeter, network, host, application, data) and decide how layers are separated (firewalls, DMZ, VLANs).
Q3 ‘More layers always means more security.’ Agree?
Not automatically — layers must be independent. If one stolen password opens every layer, the layers fail together.
1.8

Malware

Malware is any software written to harm a system. A virus attaches itself to a host file and needs the user to run it; a worm spreads across the network by itself; a Trojan pretends to be useful software; ransomware encrypts your files and demands payment. Each has a matching defence: anti-malware scanning and not opening untrusted files for viruses, prompt patching and network segmentation for worms, user awareness for Trojans, and offline backups for ransomware. The short-answer question asks for two types and how to mitigate each — name the type, say how it spreads, then give its defence.

Board
▸ Virus needs a host and a user click; a worm spreads by itself. Ask for one mitigation each.
Ask the room
Q1 What does a worm do that a virus cannot?
Spread by itself across the network. A virus needs a host file and usually a user action to run.
Q2 Give one mitigation for a virus and one for a worm.
Virus: anti-malware scanning plus not opening untrusted attachments. Worm: prompt patching and firewalls/segmentation to block its spread.
2.1

Switch attacks

A switch learns which MAC address sits on which port and sends each frame only where it needs to go — that is what makes it more secure than a hub. The Week 2 table names five attacks on it. MAC flooding fills the address table with fake MACs until the switch gives up and floods every frame to every port, like a hub, so the attacker can sniff everything. MAC impersonation copies a victim’s MAC; ARP poisoning forges IP-to-MAC answers; port mirroring and network taps copy traffic out. Each has a defence: limit MACs per port, one port per MAC, an ARP detection appliance, and physical security for mirror ports and taps.

Board
∣MAC table∣>capacity⇒switch→hub|\text{MAC table}| > \text{capacity} \Rightarrow \text{switch} \to \text{hub}∣MAC table∣>capacity⇒switch→hub
Once the address table overflows, the switch behaves like a hub and broadcasts everything.
Ask the room
Q1 Fill a switch’s address table with fake MACs. What does the switch turn into, and what’s the defence?
A hub — it floods every frame to every port, so the attacker can sniff. Defence: port security limiting MACs per port.
2.2

ARP poisoning → DNS poisoning

ARP is how a device on a local network finds the MAC address behind an IP address — and it trusts any reply it hears. In ARP poisoning the attacker sends forged replies: to the victim “I am the gateway”, to the gateway “I am the victim”. Both now send their traffic through the attacker, a man-in-the-middle position. From there the attacker can answer the victim’s DNS lookups with fake addresses (DNS cache poisoning), sending a bank’s name to a look-alike site. Defences: an ARP detection appliance (the slide’s answer), static ARP entries for critical devices, and validating DNS responses.

Board
ARP(IPgw)↦MACattacker\text{ARP}(IP_{gw}) \mapsto MAC_{attacker}ARP(IPgw​)↦MACattacker​
The gateway’s IP address now maps to the attacker’s MAC address.
Ask the room
Q1 If you sit between a victim and the gateway via ARP poisoning, what else can you fake?
DNS replies — so the victim is sent to a fake site even when typing the right name (ARP → DNS cache poisoning).
Q2 Why does ARP poisoning work at all?
ARP has no authentication; devices accept unsolicited replies and overwrite their IP-to-MAC cache.
2.3

Broadcast storms

A broadcast frame goes to every port. If two switches are joined by two cables, a broadcast goes out one cable, comes back the other, and is re-broadcast forever — multiplying each lap until the network chokes. That is a broadcast storm. Loop protection (in practice the Spanning Tree Protocol) detects the redundant path and blocks it, so no loop can form. VLANs only shrink the area a storm hits and network separation limits attack spread; neither removes the loop. The exam answer is loop protection.

Board
▸ Draw a two-switch loop and trace one broadcast frame round it forever.
Ask the room
Q1 Two cables between the same two switches. What happens to one broadcast?
It loops forever and multiplies — a broadcast storm that can bring the network down.
Q2 Which feature prevents broadcast storms, and how?
Loop protection (e.g. Spanning Tree Protocol), which detects redundant paths and blocks one so no loop forms.
2.4

Proxy servers

A proxy server is a stand-in that makes requests on the user’s behalf: your browser asks the proxy, the proxy fetches the page from the web server using its own IP address, then passes it back. Because it keeps a cache, repeat requests are answered locally — faster for users and cheaper in bandwidth. It also hides internal addresses from the outside world and can filter what users reach. A load balancer is different: it spreads incoming traffic across several servers. Watch the exam’s “advantages of proxies” question: the correct pick is increased speed while reducing costs.

Board
Client→Proxy→Server\text{Client} \rightarrow \text{Proxy} \rightarrow \text{Server}Client→Proxy→Server
The client talks to the proxy, and the proxy talks to the server on its behalf.
Ask the room
Q1 Name one thing a proxy can do that a router cannot.
Inspect and cache application requests (e.g. web pages) and filter by content — it acts on behalf of the user.
Q2 Fill the blank: ‘A ______ processes requests on behalf of the user.’ Why not a load balancer?
Proxy server. A load balancer spreads incoming requests across servers; it does not act on the user’s behalf.
Q3 How can a proxy increase speed AND reduce cost at once?
Caching: repeated requests are served locally — faster responses and less internet bandwidth paid for.
Q4 Does a proxy hide the user from the destination server?
Yes — the server sees the proxy’s address, not the user’s.
Q5 Proxy vs firewall: which reads inside the web request?
The proxy (application level). A basic packet-filtering firewall only looks at headers.
Q6 ‘All are correct’ is offered for a proxy’s advantages. When is that the safe pick?
Only if every option is true. Here the true advantage is increased speed with reduced cost — check the options, don’t default to ‘all’.
2.5

VPN

A VPN lets authorised users treat an unsecured public network as if it were a private one. Everything between the remote device and the organisation’s network is encrypted inside a tunnel, so the café owner sees only scrambled traffic to the VPN endpoint. Two types appear on the slides: remote-access (one user to the LAN) and site-to-site (whole offices joined over the internet). A VLAN is not the same thing — it only splits one organisation’s own local network into separate segments. The exam’s VPN question has ‘All of the above combined’ as a trap.

Board
tunnel=Ek(packet) over the public net\text{tunnel} = E_k(\text{packet}) \text{ over the public net}tunnel=Ek​(packet) over the public net
The whole packet travels encrypted inside a tunnel across the public network.
Ask the room
Q1 Café Wi-Fi: what does a VPN hide from the café owner?
The contents and real destination of your traffic — it is encrypted inside a tunnel to the VPN endpoint.
Q2 VPN vs VLAN — which lets you use a public network as if it were private?
VPN. A VLAN logically separates a local network; it does not secure traffic over the public internet.
2.6

DMZ, flood guard, port security, NAC

These are the perimeter and switch-hardening tools of Week 2. A DMZ is a semi-exposed zone between the internet and the internal network where public-facing servers live, so a breach there does not hand over the inside. Flood guard limits floods of traffic (half-open connections, MAC floods) so devices stay available. Port security restricts which and how many MAC addresses can use a switch port, and disables unused ports. NAC checks users and devices before they join — failing devices go to a quarantine network. Each quiz question tests one of these definitions.

Board
▸ Sketch internet | DMZ | internal on the board and place three servers.
Ask the room
Q1 Where would you put the public web server, and why not inside?
In the DMZ — so if it is breached, the attacker is still outside the internal network.
Q2 What is a flood guard protecting against?
Floods of traffic (e.g. SYN or MAC floods) — it limits rates so the device stays available.
Q3 Port security: what does it restrict?
Which/how many MAC addresses may use a switch port, blocking MAC flooding and unknown devices.
Q4 NAC checks three things before letting a laptop in. Name them.
Who the user is (authentication), what they may access (authorisation), and whether the device is healthy (patched, antivirus).
3.1

Intruder classes

Week 3 names three classes of intruder. A masquerader is an outsider using someone else’s identity — a stolen login. A misfeasor is a legitimate insider who misuses or exceeds their access. A clandestine user seizes supervisory control to dodge auditing and detection. Insiders are among the hardest attacks to detect because their access is genuine and their activity looks normal. Statistical detection is good against masqueraders but weak against misfeasors — they behave like ordinary users.

Board
▸ Hold up a staff card: “stolen” = masquerader, “mine but misused” = misfeasor.
Ask the room
Q1 An employee misuses access they genuinely have. Which class?
Misfeasor — legitimate user, abusing or exceeding their access.
Q2 Masquerader vs clandestine user?
Masquerader: an outsider using someone else’s identity. Clandestine user: seizes supervisory control to evade auditing and detection.
Q3 Why are insider attacks among the hardest to detect and prevent?
Insiders already hold legitimate access and know the systems, so their activity looks normal — True.
3.2

Stateful vs packet-filtering firewalls

A packet-filtering firewall judges each packet on its own against a rule list — source, destination, port — with no memory of what came before. A stateful inspection firewall also keeps a state table of active connections, including TCP sequence numbers, so it knows whether a packet belongs to a conversation it already allowed. That lets it drop a ‘reply’ nobody asked for. The exam wording — examines headers at all layers and remembers relationships between packets — describes the stateful firewall. ‘Relational firewall’ is an invented distractor.

Board
filter: f(pkt)stateful: f(pkt,state)\text{filter: } f(\text{pkt}) \qquad \text{stateful: } f(\text{pkt}, \text{state})filter: f(pkt)stateful: f(pkt,state)
A packet filter decides from the packet alone; a stateful firewall also uses connection state.
Ask the room
Q1 Why can a stateful firewall reject a reply nobody asked for?
It remembers outgoing connections; an ‘answer’ with no matching request is dropped.
Q2 Which firewall examines headers at all layers and remembers relationships between packets?
Stateful inspection firewall. A packet filter judges each packet alone; a circuit-level gateway works at session level.
3.3

Default policy and bastion host

A firewall needs a default for anything its rules do not mention. Default discard (deny) means whatever is not expressly permitted is blocked — cautious, so businesses and government use it. Default forward (allow) means whatever is not expressly forbidden passes — convenient but risky. A bastion host is the single, heavily hardened machine deliberately exposed to attack, a critical strong point in the network’s security. It runs only essential services, so there is little to attack.

Board
∀t: deny(t) unless t∈Allowed\forall t:\ \text{deny}(t)\ \text{unless}\ t \in \text{Allowed}∀t: deny(t) unless t∈Allowed
For all traffic: deny it unless it is explicitly allowed.
Ask the room
Q1 Default deny or default allow: which would a bank choose?
Default deny (discard) — anything not explicitly allowed is blocked. Safer, at the cost of convenience.
Q2 What is a bastion host?
A hardened system that is a critical strong point in the network’s security, exposed to attack and built to withstand it.
3.4

Rule-based vs anomaly detection

Intrusion detection works two ways. Rule-based (signature-based) detection matches behaviour against a set of rules or known attack patterns — fast and precise, but blind to brand-new attacks. Statistical anomaly detection first learns what normal behaviour looks like, then flags departures from it; threshold detection (counting events) and profile-based detection are its forms. The exam definition — rules or attack patterns used to decide if behaviour is due to an intruder — is rule-based. Statistics-based is good against masqueraders, weak against misfeasors.

Board
rule: x∈Patternsanomaly: ∣x−μ∣>kσ\text{rule: } x \in \text{Patterns} \qquad \text{anomaly: } |x-\mu| > k\sigmarule: x∈Patternsanomaly: ∣x−μ∣>kσ
Rule-based: x matches a known pattern. Anomaly: x is more than k standard deviations from normal.
Ask the room
Q1 Rule-based or anomaly — which can catch a brand-new attack?
Anomaly detection — it flags departures from normal behaviour, even with no known pattern.
Q2 ‘A set of rules or attack patterns used to decide if behaviour is due to an intruder.’ Name it.
Rule-based detection.
Q3 Threshold detection: rule-based or anomaly?
Statistical anomaly — it counts events and alarms when a threshold for ‘normal’ is exceeded.
Q4 Biggest weakness of rule-based detection?
It misses attacks that match no existing rule (zero-days) until the rules are updated.
3.5

IDS outcomes

Every IDS decision has two parts: did it raise an alarm (positive or negative), and was it right (true or false)? A true positive is an alarm for a real intrusion; a false positive is an alarm with no intruder; a false negative is a real intrusion that goes unnoticed; a true negative is correct silence. False positives waste analysts’ time and train them to ignore alarms. False negatives are the most dangerous, because the attacker is inside and nobody knows. The quiz scenario — alarm, then no intruder found — is a false positive.

Board
TP=alarm∧intrusionFP=alarm∧¬intrusion\text{TP} = \text{alarm} \wedge \text{intrusion} \qquad \text{FP} = \text{alarm} \wedge \neg\text{intrusion}TP=alarm∧intrusionFP=alarm∧¬intrusion
True positive: alarm and a real intrusion. False positive: alarm with no intrusion.
Ask the room
Q1 Alarm rings, no intruder. What is that called?
A false positive.
Q2 The IDS correctly flags a real intrusion. Name the outcome.
True positive.
Q3 Which outcome is the most dangerous, and why?
False negative — a real intrusion goes unnoticed.
3.6

Salt and pepper

Passwords are stored as hashes, not plain text. Without salt, two users with the same password get the same hash, and an attacker can use precomputed (rainbow) tables. A salt is a random value generated when the password is first set, stored openly beside the hash; it makes every user’s hash unique and defeats precomputed tables. A pepper is a secret value added to every hash and stored separately from the database (for example in application configuration), so a stolen database alone cannot be cracked. The exam’s salt initialisation answer: generate a random number when loading the password.

Board
stored=(s, H(s ∥ pw ∥ p))s random per user, p secret\begin{gathered}\text{stored} = \big(s,\ H(s \,\|\, pw \,\|\, p)\big) \\ s\ \text{random per user},\ p\ \text{secret}\end{gathered}stored=(s, H(s∥pw∥p))s random per user, p secret​
Store the salt s and the hash of salt, password and pepper. The salt is random per user; the pepper is kept secret.
Ask the room
Q1 Two users both pick ‘password123’. Without salt, what does the attacker see?
Identical hashes — crack one, crack both, and precomputed rainbow tables work.
Q2 Where are salt and pepper stored?
Salt: stored with the hash, per user (not secret). Pepper: a secret kept separately (e.g. in application config), not in the database.
Q3 How should the system initialise a user’s salt?
Generate a random number when the password is first set/loaded — never ask the user for it.
4.1

Correctness

Correctness is the most basic promise a cipher makes: decrypting with the same key must give back exactly the original message, for every key and every message. Without it the receiver could not read anything, so the scheme is useless however strong it looks. The subscript k matters — it says the same key is used on both sides, which is what makes the scheme symmetric. This line is the starting point for everything else in Week 4: once correctness holds, the question becomes how hard it is to decrypt without k. It is a good opener on the board.

Board
∀k,∀m: Dk(Ek(m))=m\forall k,\forall m:\ D_k(E_k(m)) = m∀k,∀m: Dk​(Ek​(m))=m
For every key k and every message m, decrypting with k what was encrypted with k gives back m.
Ask the room
Q1 If decryption did not give m back, what would be broken?
The scheme’s correctness — the receiver could not recover the message, so it is not a usable cipher, however ‘secure’.
4.2

Kerckhoffs’s principle

Kerckhoffs’s principle: a cipher must stay secure even when the attacker knows everything about it except the key. Algorithms leak — they are reverse-engineered, published or sold — so security cannot rest on hiding them. That is why AES is completely public and still trusted: experts worldwide have attacked it openly and failed. If the key leaks, the strongest algorithm gives no protection. In Singpass terms, its HTTPS traffic runs on public AES; the protection is the session key, not secrecy about how AES works. This idea appears on every past paper as the key-secrecy question.

Board
c=Ek(m),E,D public, k secretc = E_k(m),\quad E, D\ \text{public},\ k\ \text{secret}c=Ek​(m),E,D public, k secret
c equals E-sub-k of m. E and D are public; only k is secret.
Ask the room
Q1 ‘AES is safe because nobody knows how it works.’ True?
False. AES is fully public; its security rests only on the secrecy of the key.
Q2 Symmetric encryption’s security depends on the secrecy of what?
The key — not the algorithm, not the plaintext.
Q3 Why publish the algorithm at all?
So experts worldwide can attack it; surviving public scrutiny is what earns trust. Hidden algorithms leak and then fail.
Q4 If the key is stolen but the algorithm is strong, are you safe?
No. With the key, anyone can decrypt — the algorithm offers no protection.
4.3

Three ways to classify a cipher

Week 4 classifies any cipher on three independent axes. Operation: substitution (replace symbols) or transposition (rearrange them). Number of keys: one shared key (symmetric) or a key pair (asymmetric). Processing: block (a fixed-size chunk at a time) or stream (one element at a time). AES is substitution-permutation, one key, block; the Caesar cipher is substitution, one key, letter by letter. For a real-time sensor, a stream cipher fits — it encrypts as data arrives with low delay — but keys must never be reused, and encryption alone does not prove integrity.

Board
{Sub,Trans}×{1 key,2 keys}×{Block,Stream}\{\text{Sub},\text{Trans}\} \times \{1\text{ key}, 2\text{ keys}\} \times \{\text{Block},\text{Stream}\}{Sub,Trans}×{1 key,2 keys}×{Block,Stream}
Every cipher is a choice of substitution or transposition, times one key or two, times block or stream.
Ask the room
Q1 Where does AES sit on the three axes?
Substitution and permutation, one key (symmetric), block.
Q2 Sender and receiver share one key. What is the scheme called?
Symmetric (also conventional, secret-key, single-key) encryption.
Q3 A real-time sensor streams data continuously. Block or stream cipher, and why?
Stream — it encrypts bit by bit with low latency and no waiting to fill blocks; but never reuse the key/IV.
Q4 Caesar cipher on the three axes?
Substitution, one key (symmetric), stream-like (letter by letter).
4.4

Brute force

Brute force means trying every possible key until one turns ciphertext into readable plaintext. The right key is equally likely to be anywhere, so on average you find it halfway: |K|/2 tries. Each extra key bit doubles the work. DES’s 56-bit key (about 7×10¹⁶ keys) fell to purpose-built hardware in the late 1990s; AES-128 has 2¹²⁸ keys, which is far beyond any computer. So key length is the defence against brute force, and the algorithm must not have shortcuts that make the search smaller.

Board
E[tries]=∣K∣2=2n−1\mathbb{E}[\text{tries}] = \tfrac{|\mathcal{K}|}{2} = 2^{n-1}E[tries]=2∣K∣​=2n−1
The expected number of tries is the size of the keyspace over two, which is two to the n minus one.
Ask the room
Q1 Why only half the keyspace on average?
The right key is equally likely to be anywhere, so on average you find it halfway through: |K|/2.
4.5

Stream key reuse

A stream cipher XORs the message with a keystream z: c = m ⊕ z. If the same keystream is ever used twice, an attacker can XOR the two ciphertexts and the keystream cancels, leaving m ⊕ m′. From there, known words in one message reveal the other. This is how WEP fell: its short initialisation vectors repeated, so keystreams repeated. The cipher (RC4) was not the main problem — key management was. Rule: never reuse a key-IV pair.

Board
c⊕c′=(m⊕z)⊕(m′⊕z)=m⊕m′c \oplus c' = (m \oplus z) \oplus (m' \oplus z) = m \oplus m'c⊕c′=(m⊕z)⊕(m′⊕z)=m⊕m′
c XOR c-prime equals m XOR m-prime: the keystream z cancels out.
Ask the room
Q1 WEP used a strong cipher. So why did it fall?
Keystream reuse — short, repeating IVs meant c ⊕ c′ = m ⊕ m′, leaking plaintext. The key management failed, not RC4.
4.6

CTR mode

Block cipher modes decide how blocks are chained. In CTR (counter) mode the cipher encrypts a counter value and XORs the result with the plaintext block: Cᵢ = Pᵢ ⊕ E_k(ctrᵢ). Each block depends only on its own counter, so blocks can be processed in parallel or out of order, and the block cipher behaves like a stream cipher. CFB mode also turns a block cipher into a stream cipher. CBC instead chains each block to the previous ciphertext, so it cannot be parallelised for encryption.

Board
Ci=Pi⊕Ek(ctri)C_i = P_i \oplus E_k(\text{ctr}_i)Ci​=Pi​⊕Ek​(ctri​)
Ciphertext block i equals plaintext block i XOR the encryption of counter i.
Ask the room
Q1 Why can CTR encrypt block 100 before block 1?
Each block uses E_k(counter_i), which depends on nothing but i — so blocks are independent and parallel. CTR and CFB both turn a block cipher into a stream cipher.
5.1

Encryption is not authentication

Encryption hides content; it does not prove the content is genuine. If blocks are encrypted independently, an attacker can reorder, remove or replay whole ciphertext blocks, and each one still decrypts cleanly to different meaning. So ‘it decrypted fine’ only shows the right key was used. To prove integrity and origin you need an authentication tag — a MAC or protected hash — checked by the receiver. Modern secure connections pair encryption with such a tag on every record. The exam statement ‘symmetric encryption alone is sufficient for authentication’ is False.

Board
Dk(c′) decrypts  ⇏  m′=mD_k(c') \text{ decrypts} \;\not\Rightarrow\; m' = mDk​(c′) decrypts⇒m′=m
That c-prime decrypts does not imply that m-prime equals m.
Ask the room
Q1 I swapped two encrypted blocks and it still decrypted. Is the message authentic?
No. Symmetric encryption alone is not sufficient for authentication — False. You need a tag (MAC/hash).
5.2

Hash vs MAC

Message authentication proves a message is unaltered and from a genuine source. The general approach: attach an authentication tag computed by a function both sides share; encrypt on top if secrecy is needed too. Two tag types. A one-way hash takes only the message (MD = H(M)), so it must be protected — by encrypting the digest or mixing in a shared secret. A MAC takes a secret key and the message (T = F(K, M)), so only key holders can make it. Message authentication is not the same as user authentication (Week 6), which verifies a person or entity.

Board
MD=H(M)vsT=F(K,M)MD = H(M) \quad\text{vs}\quad T = F(K, M)MD=H(M)vsT=F(K,M)
The digest equals H of M, versus the tag equals F of K and M.
Ask the room
Q1 If a hash needs no key, how can it prove who sent the message?
On its own it can’t — protect it with a shared key, a private key, or a secret value; or use a MAC, which takes K and M.
5.3

Hash functions and their properties

A hash function takes input of any length and produces a fixed-length digest. Because there are far more possible inputs than outputs, many inputs must share each output — so in principle a hash is reversible, just computationally infeasible for a secure one. Three properties matter: preimage resistance (can’t find an input for a given hash), second preimage resistance (can’t find another input with the same hash as a given one), and collision resistance (can’t find any two inputs that match). Collision resistance is the hardest to keep, because the attacker chooses both inputs.

Board
H:{0,1}∗→{0,1}nH: \{0,1\}^{*} \rightarrow \{0,1\}^{n}H:{0,1}∗→{0,1}n
H maps bit strings of any length to bit strings of length n.
Ask the room
Q1 If outputs are shorter than inputs, must collisions exist?
Yes (pigeonhole). So a secure hash is ‘reversible in principle but difficult’ — True.
5.4

XOR hash collision

The exam’s toy hash splits the message into 8-bit blocks and XORs them together. For 0100 1011 1101 0011: 0100 1011 ⊕ 1101 0011 = 1001 1000. XOR is commutative — A ⊕ B = B ⊕ A — so swapping the blocks gives exactly the same hash: a different message, same digest, a collision. You can also flip the same bit position in both blocks; the flips cancel. The wrong options give 0111 0110 and 1011 0100. This question has appeared on all four papers, so drill the bit arithmetic.

Board
h=B1⊕B2⊕⋯⊕Bm,B1⊕B2=B2⊕B1h = B_1 \oplus B_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus B_m,\quad B_1 \oplus B_2 = B_2 \oplus B_1h=B1​⊕B2​⊕⋯⊕Bm​,B1​⊕B2​=B2​⊕B1​
h equals B-one XOR B-two XOR … B-m; and B-one XOR B-two equals B-two XOR B-one.
Ask the room
Q1 Give me a different message with the same XOR hash as 0100 1011 1101 0011.
1101 0011 0100 1011 — swap the blocks; XOR doesn’t care about order. Both hash to 1001 1000.
Q2 What does 0100 1011 ⊕ 1101 0011 give?
1001 1000 — bits that differ give 1, bits that match give 0.
Q3 Does 0001 1101 0110 1011 collide with it?
No: 0001 1101 ⊕ 0110 1011 = 0111 0110 ≠ 1001 1000.
Q4 Name a second trick (besides swapping) that makes an XOR-hash collision.
Flip the same bit position in two blocks — the two flips cancel. E.g. 1100 1011 0101 0011.
5.5

Birthday paradox

The birthday paradox: in a room of 23 people, there is better than a 50% chance two share a birthday, yet to match one specific birthday you need about 253 people. The same maths applies to hashes. Finding any collision in an n-bit hash takes about 2^(n/2) attempts, while matching a given hash (a preimage) takes about 2^n. So a 128-bit hash gives only about 64 bits of collision resistance. That is why hash outputs keep getting longer, and why SHA-1’s 160 bits are no longer considered enough.

Board
1−∏i=022365−i365≈0.507;2n/2≪2n1 - \prod_{i=0}^{22}\tfrac{365-i}{365} \approx 0.507;\quad 2^{n/2} \ll 2^{n}1−∏i=022​365365−i​≈0.507;2n/2≪2n
One minus the product, for i from 0 to 22, of 365 minus i over 365, is about 0.507; and two to the n over two is far smaller than two to the n.
Ask the room
Q1 How many people before two probably share a birthday?
23 (≈50.7%). Matching one specific birthday needs about 253 — collisions are far cheaper than preimages.
5.6

HMAC

A plain hash has no key, so anyone can recompute it on a forged message. HMAC turns a hash into a MAC by mixing in a secret key twice: the key, padded and XORed with an inner pattern (ipad), is hashed with the message; that result is hashed again with the key XORed with an outer pattern (opad). The nested structure blocks attacks that work on a simple hash of key plus message. HMAC is mandatory in TLS and IPsec. Authenticator codes (one-time passwords) are built on the same idea: HMAC of a shared seed and the time.

Board
H((K+ ⁣⊕opad) ∥ H((K+ ⁣⊕ipad) ∥ M))H\big((K^{+}\!\oplus \text{opad}) \,\|\, H((K^{+}\!\oplus \text{ipad}) \,\|\, M)\big)H((K+⊕opad)∥H((K+⊕ipad)∥M))
H of: K-plus XOR opad, joined to H of K-plus XOR ipad joined to M.
Ask the room
Q1 Why not just hash the key and the message once?
Simple key+message hashes are open to attacks like length extension; HMAC’s nested double hash with ipad/opad closes them.
5.7

Public-key: which key does what

Public-key cryptography gives each user a key pair: a public key anyone may have and a private key only the owner holds. What one key encrypts, only the other decrypts — and which key you use decides what you get. Encrypt with the recipient’s public key and only they can read it (secrecy). Encrypt with your own private key and anyone can check it came from you (proof of origin) — that is a digital signature. So the private key’s most important use is generating digital signatures; verifying uses the public key.

Board
Dsk(Epk(m))=mD_{sk}(E_{pk}(m)) = mDsk​(Epk​(m))=m
Decrypting with the secret key what was encrypted with the public key gives back m.
Ask the room
Q1 Alice wants only Bob to read it. Whose key, which one?
Bob’s public key — only Bob’s private key decrypts it.
Q2 What is the most important use of the private key: generate or verify signatures?
Generate digital signatures. Verifying uses the public key.
5.8

Diffie-Hellman

Diffie-Hellman (1976) lets two parties agree a secret key over an open channel without ever sending it. Both share public values q and α; each picks a private number, sends α to that power mod q, then raises what they receive to their own private number — both reach α^(ab) mod q. An eavesdropper sees only the public values and cannot recover the key, because that needs the discrete logarithm. But nothing proves who you exchanged with, so a man-in-the-middle can run one exchange with each side. Key exchange alone does not prevent MITM.

Board
K=(αb)a=(αa)b=αab mod qK = (\alpha^{b})^{a} = (\alpha^{a})^{b} = \alpha^{ab} \bmod qK=(αb)a=(αa)b=αabmodq
K equals alpha to the b, raised to the a, equals alpha to the a, raised to the b, equals alpha to the a-b, mod q.
Ask the room
Q1 Can an attacker in the middle run two exchanges at once?
Yes — Diffie-Hellman authenticates no one, so it does NOT avoid man-in-the-middle (False). Add certificates or signatures.
5.9

RSA

RSA (1978) builds keys from two large primes p and q. n = pq is public; φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1) is secret; the public exponent e and private d satisfy e·d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)). Encrypt with C = Mᵉ mod n; decrypt with M = Cᵈ mod n. Worked example: p = 11, q = 17, n = 187, φ = 160, e = 7, d = 23 because 7 × 23 = 161 ≡ 1. Security rests on factoring: multiplying primes is easy, but recovering p and q from a huge n is infeasible.

Board
ed≡1(modφ(n)),C=Me, M=Cd mod ned \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)},\quad C = M^{e},\ M = C^{d} \bmod ned≡1(modφ(n)),C=Me, M=Cdmodn
e times d is congruent to 1 modulo phi of n; C equals M to the e, and M equals C to the d, mod n.
Ask the room
Q1 Factor 187. Now factor a 600-digit number.
187 = 11 × 17 in seconds; the large one is infeasible. RSA’s security rests on that gap.
5.10

Digital signatures

A digital signature is message authentication with non-repudiation. The signer hashes the message and encrypts the hash with their private key; anyone verifies by decrypting with the signer’s public key and comparing hashes. A match proves the message is unaltered and that only the private-key holder could have signed — so they cannot later deny it. A MAC cannot do this, because both parties hold the same key. Sign with Singpass (2020) uses this: documents are signed from the phone, with certificates from the National Certification Authority.

Board
S=H(M)d mod n,accept iff Se mod n=H(M)S = H(M)^{d} \bmod n,\quad \text{accept iff } S^{e} \bmod n = H(M)S=H(M)dmodn,accept iff Semodn=H(M)
S equals H of M to the d, mod n; accept if and only if S to the e, mod n, equals H of M.
Ask the room
Q1 What does a signature give that a MAC cannot?
Non-repudiation — only the signer’s private key could have made it; with a MAC, either key holder could.
Q2 Encrypting a hash with your private key is essentially what?
A digital signature — the main use of the private key.
6.1

Counting symmetric keys

With symmetric keys, every pair of people who want to talk privately needs their own key. For n people that is n choose 2 = n(n−1)/2 keys: you don’t need a key with yourself, and the A–B key is the same as B–A. Fifteen people need 105 keys; a thousand need almost half a million. Each key must be delivered secretly and replaced regularly. This explosion is why large networks use a Key Distribution Centre or public-key methods instead of pairwise keys.

Board

N equals n choose 2, which is n times n minus one over two; fifteen people need 105 keys.
Ask the room
Q1 15 people, one key per pair. How many keys?
15 × 14 / 2 = 105.
Q2 Why not 15 × 15 = 225?
You don’t need a key with yourself (−15), and the A–B key is the same as B–A (÷2).
6.2

Key distribution: symmetric vs public-key

Symmetric and public-key systems have different distribution problems. Symmetric: both sides must hold the same secret key, and no one else may see it — delivered physically, encrypted under an old key, or handed out by a trusted Key Distribution Centre that shares a permanent key with each party. Public-key: the public key can be shared openly, so secrecy is not the issue — authenticity is: is this key really Bob’s? That is solved with certificates signed by a Certificate Authority. In practice public-key methods are often used to deliver a symmetric session key, as in the TLS handshake.

Board
▸ Hand one student a sealed envelope (symmetric) and pin a padlock on the wall (public key).
Ask the room
Q1 You can shout your public key across the room. Can you shout a symmetric key?
No — anyone hearing it can decrypt. Symmetric keys need a secret channel or a key distribution centre.
Q2 Public-key systems still have a distribution problem. What is it?
Trust: proving a public key really belongs to its claimed owner — solved with certificates (CA/PKI).
6.3

Permanent and session keys

Keys come in a hierarchy. A permanent (master) key is shared long-term between each party and the Key Distribution Centre. When A wants to talk to B, the KDC generates a one-time session key and sends it to each, encrypted under their permanent key. The session key encrypts the actual data and is then thrown away. This limits exposure: each permanent key is used rarely, and if a session key leaks, only that session is affected. Exam phrasing: a permanent key is used to distribute session keys.

Board
Ksession→ EKperm each partyK_{\text{session}} \xrightarrow{\ E_{K_{\text{perm}}}\ } \text{each party}Ksession​ EKperm​​ ​each party
The session key travels encrypted under each party’s permanent key.
Ask the room
Q1 Why not use the permanent key for every message?
Each use exposes it to analysis; short-lived session keys limit the damage if one is broken.
Q2 Fill: ‘A ______ key is used to distribute ______ keys.’
Permanent, session.
Q3 Who issues the session key in a symmetric scheme?
The Key Distribution Centre (KDC), encrypted under each party’s permanent (master) key.
Q4 A session key leaks. What is exposed?
Only that session’s traffic — the permanent key and other sessions stay safe.
6.4

Kerberos tickets and freshness

Kerberos (MIT) gives single authentication across a network. The Authentication Server checks the user once and issues a ticket-granting ticket; the Ticket Granting Server then issues service tickets. Each ticket carries a timestamp and a lifetime: v4 hard-coded about 21 hours, v5 allows explicit start and end times. A copied ticket is useless once it expires, which stops replay. Nonces — values used once — prove that each response is fresh. Tickets are also protected by encryption under the server’s key and an authenticator only the real client can produce.

Ask the room
Q1 I copy your ticket today. Why can’t I use it next week?
Its timestamp and lifetime expire — this stops replay attacks.
Q2 What does a nonce guarantee in a Kerberos v5 exchange?
Freshness — the response matches this request, not one replayed by an attacker.
Q3 Name another way to protect tickets besides lifetime.
Encryption under the service’s key, plus an authenticator (timestamp, client address) only the real client can produce.
Q4 Why does the TGT exist at all?
So you authenticate once with the AS; the TGS then issues service tickets without re-sending your password.
6.5

Certificates and X.509

Anyone can publish a public key and claim it is someone else’s — that is public-key cryptography’s weak point. A public-key certificate fixes it: the key plus the owner’s ID, signed by a trusted Certificate Authority. To check a certificate you verify the CA’s signature with the CA’s public key. X.509 is the ITU standard that defines the certificate format. Your browser trusts a small set of root CAs pre-installed on the device, and every website certificate chains back to one of them.

A certificate is the CA’s signature over the identity joined to its public key.

Q1 Who signs the CA’s own certificate?
The root CA signs its own (self-signed); trust in it is pre-installed in browsers and operating systems.
Q2 Digital certificates are related to what?
Establishing the authenticity of public keys.
Q3 What standard defines the certificate format?
X.509 (ITU-T).
Q4 What is used to verify a certificate?
The CA’s public key — it checks the CA’s signature on the certificate.
Q5 A public-key certificate is signed by whom?
The Certificate Authority, with the CA’s private key.
6.6

User authentication

User authentication has two steps: identification (claim who you are) and verification (prove it). There are four general means of proof. Something you know: a password or PIN. Something you have: a token or card. Something you are: static biometrics such as a fingerprint or face. Something you do: dynamic biometrics such as voice, handwriting or typing rhythm. Combining factors from different categories gives multi-factor authentication. Message authentication proves where data came from; it is not the same as user authentication.

Ask the room
Q1 Fingerprint and typing rhythm: which is ‘are’ and which is ‘do’?
Fingerprint = something you are (static biometric); typing rhythm = something you do (dynamic biometric).
Q2 Name the four general means of authenticating a user.
Something you know, have, are, and do.
Q3 Which is ‘something you are’: fingerprint, handwriting, keys you created, answers to questions?
Fingerprint. Handwriting is ‘do’; keys are ‘have’; answers are ‘know’.
Q4 User authentication has two steps. Name them.
Identification (claim who you are) and verification (prove it).
Q5 Can message authentication also be used for user authentication?
Yes (True) — e.g. a signed or MACed challenge proves the user holds the key.
6.7

Single sign-on

Single sign-on lets a user log in once and then reach many applications without re-entering a password. Three parties: the user, the service providers (the apps), and a trusted identity provider. The user opens App A, is redirected to the identity provider, logs in there, and receives a signed token that App A verifies. Visiting App B, the identity provider sees the existing session and issues a new token with no second login. Apps never see the password, which improves security — but the identity provider becomes a single point of failure.

7.1

NAC enforcement methods

Network Access Control decides whether a device may join the local network, checking the user, their permissions and the device’s health (patches, anti-malware). Its three components are the Access Requestor (the device), the Network Access Server (the gatekeeper) and the Policy Server (the rules). Enforcement happens through IEEE 802.1X port-based authentication — by far the most common method — VLAN assignment (failing devices land in a quarantine VLAN), firewalls, and DHCP management (withholding or restricting IP addresses). A VPN is a remote-access tool for reaching the network from outside, not a NAC enforcement method.

Ask the room
Q1 Which of 802.1X, VLANs, DHCP management and VPNs is NOT a NAC enforcement method?
VPNs. A VPN gives remote access over the internet; NAC controls devices joining the local network. The other three are standard NAC enforcement methods.
Q2 A laptop fails its health check. Where does NAC usually put it?
On a quarantine network (a restricted VLAN) until it is patched or cleaned.
Q3 Which NAC enforcement method is by far the most common?
IEEE 802.1X — the device must authenticate at the switch port or Wi-Fi association before it can send normal traffic.
7.2

802.1X / EAP roles and RADIUS

802.1X authentication has three roles. The supplicant is the device asking to join (your laptop). The authenticator is the switch or access point at the door; it relays messages but does not decide. The authentication server sits behind it, checks the credentials and tells the authenticator yes or no — RADIUS is the real protocol that fills this role. EAP (RFC 3748) is the framework that carries different authentication methods between supplicant and server. RADIUS can also send back extras such as a VLAN number, but its core job is backend authentication.

Q1 Laptop, Wi-Fi access point, RADIUS server: which is the supplicant, authenticator and authentication server?
Laptop = supplicant; access point = authenticator; RADIUS = authentication server.
Q2 Does the authenticator check the password itself?
No. It relays the exchange and enforces the result; RADIUS, the backend authentication server, makes the decision.
7.3

Blockchain

A blockchain is a shared ledger copied across many independent computers. Each block holds a hash of the previous block, so changing an old record changes its hash and breaks every block after it — the network rejects it. That gives immutability: confirmed records cannot be quietly edited. There is no central authority; the participants agree on new blocks together — decentralisation. High fees and slow processing are drawbacks of some blockchains, not advantages. Note: the model answer key flags blockchain as outside the slide decks.

Immutability and decentralisation. Centralised control is the opposite of the design.
8.1

TLS session and the Handshake protocol

TLS (the successor to SSL) secures data between applications over TCP. A TLS connection is one transient client-server link; a TLS session is the agreed set of security parameters, which several connections can share so the expensive negotiation is not repeated. The session is created by the Handshake protocol: client and server agree on algorithms, authenticate (the server with its certificate), and establish keys. The handshake runs in four phases: security capabilities, server authentication and key exchange, client authentication and key exchange, finish. The Record protocol then gives confidentiality and message integrity.

The Handshake protocol.

A session can be shared by many connections, avoiding a new negotiation each time; a connection is transient.

Handshake.
8.2

SSL/TLS secures web traffic

Plain HTTP sends web pages in clear text, so anyone on the path can read or alter them. SSL/TLS sits underneath HTTP and turns it into HTTPS: it encrypts the traffic (confidentiality), checks it has not been altered (message integrity), and proves the website’s identity with a certificate (authentication). FTP and SMTP are unrelated protocols with no built-in encryption. The Record protocol’s two services are confidentiality and message integrity. Every Singpass login page runs over TLS.

8.3

SMTP limitations

SMTP moves email between mail servers. It was designed for plain 7-bit text, so it cannot send executable or binary files without extra encoding (such as MIME), and servers may reject messages over a size limit. Sending text is exactly what it was built for, so ‘cannot transmit text data’ is NOT a limitation — that is the exam answer. Plain SMTP also has no encryption of its own. 9.1

Securing wireless transmission

Wireless signals travel through walls, so anyone nearby can listen. The slide pairs threats with countermeasures: eavesdropping → signal hiding and encryption; altering or inserting messages → encryption and authentication; disruption → site surveys. Signal hiding means turning off SSID broadcasting, giving SSIDs cryptic names, reducing signal strength to what coverage needs, and placing access points away from windows and outer walls. WEP is broken, open guest access and default router settings are mistakes — so the exam’s essential step is disabling SSID broadcast.

9.2

Wireless threats and man-in-the-middle

Wireless networks face extra threats: accidental and malicious association, ad hoc networks, MAC address spoofing (identity theft), network injection, denial of service, and man-in-the-middle attacks. In a wireless MITM attack, an intermediate device sits between the endpoint and the access point; both believe they are talking directly to each other while the attacker relays — and can read or alter — everything. Wireless is especially exposed because anyone in range can transmit. Mobile devices’ limited memory and processing also make them vulnerable to denial-of-service attacks.

Ask the room
Q1 An attacker convinces a user and an access point that they are talking directly. Which attack?
Man-in-the-middle.
Q2 MAC spoofing vs man-in-the-middle — what’s the difference?
MAC spoofing fakes one device’s identity; MITM deceives both sides at once and relays between them.
Q3 Why are phones and IoT devices vulnerable to DoS?
Their limited memory and processing resources are easily exhausted.
9.3

Malicious association (evil twin)

In malicious association, an attacker sets up a wireless device that pretends to be a legitimate access point — the café’s Wi-Fi name with a stronger signal. Users connect to it, and the operator steals their passwords. With those stolen credentials the attacker then penetrates the real wired network through the legitimate access point. Accidental association is the innocent version: overlapping signals connect you to a neighbour’s network by mistake. Defences include mutual authentication (802.1X/WPA2-Enterprise) and teaching users to verify networks.

Malicious association.
Q2 Malicious vs accidental association?
Malicious: an attacker’s fake AP. Accidental: overlapping signals connect you to a neighbouring network by mistake.
Q3 What does the attacker do with the stolen passwords?
Logs in through the real, legitimate access point and penetrates the wired network.
10.1

IPsec functional areas and services

IPsec secures traffic at the IP layer, so every application above it is protected without changes. It covers three functional areas: authentication, confidentiality and key management. Two protocols deliver it: the Authentication Header (AH) for integrity and origin authentication, and the Encapsulating Security Payload (ESP) for encryption and optionally authentication; keys come from Internet Key Exchange (IKE). Its services include access control, connectionless integrity, data origin authentication, rejection of replayed packets, confidentiality and limited traffic-flow confidentiality. Its two principal features: encryption and authentication.

Authentication, confidentiality and key management.

Encryption and authentication.
10.2

IPsec modes, and why (not) use IPsec

IPsec has two modes. Transport mode protects the packet’s payload and leaves the original IP header visible — used end-to-end between two hosts that both run IPsec. Tunnel mode wraps the entire original packet, header included, inside a new packet — used between gateways for site-to-site VPNs, and it hides the original addresses. ESP supports both modes. Why employ IPsec: it protects all traffic at the network layer, transparently to applications and users. Why it is not used more: configuration and key management are complex, it adds overhead, and NAT and firewalls can interfere — so TLS is often simpler for single applications.
